Pit strategy

Brazilian Grand Prix, 71 laps, a pit stop costs 22.2 s. Qualifying has not been run, so each simulated race draws its own grid from the pace forecast. Each square is a one-stop plan, played 1,500 times for the chosen driver while the rest of the field follows typical strategies. A stronger colour means more expected points. Move over the squares, or use the arrow keys.

Same answer for all 3 drivers: 1 stop, Soft then Hard, first stop around lap 30. The fitted wear is nearly the same for the soft and the medium, so the faster soft tyre wins as the start. Real soft tyres fall off more sharply than straight-line wear can show, so the optimiser probably favours softs and long stints more than it should.

Checked on 552 drivers in 72 past races: the simulator's view of a plan moves with how far ahead the driver finished, but real gains were about 27% of the simulated ones (95% interval 9% to 49%). Read the ranking of plans, not the size of the gain.

Undercut and overcut. Two cars level on pace, one second apart: stopping on the same lap gives the car behind 36% to finish ahead; one lap earlier 48%, two laps earlier 63%, one lap later 22%. An undercut helps and an overcut does not. The simulator has no tyre warm-up on the out-lap and no traffic on rejoining, so it probably overstates the undercut and cannot show an overcut. Checks

Every result above comes with the test it was put through. The simulator was tested on past races it had not seen, from 80 dry races since 2023 (wet races, 7, are left out).

As a podium forecaster, the simulator is worse than the podium model and not clearly different from a formula on grid position alone. Brier score difference to the podium model: +0.0062 (95% interval +0.0020 to +0.0103). To the grid formula: +0.0045 (95% interval -0.0019 to +0.0110). Negative means the simulator is better. Intervals come from resampling whole races, on the seasons it was not tuned on.

Replaying a real race with its real stops and retirements, and the race's own pace, puts finishers in nearly the right order. With pace forecast from earlier races it loses accuracy, so the pace forecast is the weak part. The simulator is a fair podium forecaster but not a better one; its use is that it produces whole races, which the podium model cannot.

How it works

  • Each lap a car's time is its pace, plus the tyre offset, plus tyre wear, plus the fuel slope, plus noise.
  • A car cannot pass the one ahead unless it is faster by a margin. Otherwise it is held behind it. A car in the pit lane is not held.
  • A pit stop costs the pit loss and changes the tyre. Under a safety car it costs less.
  • Cars can retire, using each driver's and team's past retirement rate, and a safety car can close up the field, using each circuit's past rate.
  • The whole field follows one typical strategy per simulated race, taken from earlier races at that circuit, with small differences in stop laps.
  • Pace is forecast from team and driver form and the qualifying gap. All fitting uses earlier races only.
  • Not modelled: rain, tyre warm-up and sudden tyre drop-off, damage, penalties during the race, team orders and the effect of fuel on tyre wear.
